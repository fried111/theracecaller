How It Works

Behind the Calls

The networks will tell you who won. The Race Caller is built to show you why, and to put my judgment on the record before a single vote is counted. Here's exactly how it works, where the numbers come from and how I'm keeping myself honest.

Built to sit alongside the networks, not replace them.

How I pick

Every race gets a winner. No hedging, no "too close to call."

1

Start with the polls

Each race starts at its polling average and the consensus of Cook Political Report, Inside Elections and Sabato's Crystal Ball.

2

Go against them only for a reason

I break from the polls when something specific points the other way: how a state's late votes usually break, county benchmarks, ratings moves, prediction markets or how polls missed there before. Those picks are flagged vs polls.

3

Lock it before the polls close

Each pick locks automatically when that state's polls close and can't be changed. Every version of the site is archived with a timestamp in a public GitHub record, so the picks are provably on the record before results come in.

Election night

Results, my calls and the math behind them.

Results

Vote counts and calls come from Associated Press–sourced reporting by major news outlets and from official state results sites. The desk checks for new results about every 15 minutes, and the website refreshes every 7 to 8 minutes through election night, with a countdown at the top of every page.

A race shows as called only when the AP or a major decision desk projects it. Never on my say-so.

My calls

Separately from the AP, I make my own calls as the votes come in. Each one is timestamped and compared with the AP's call, so you can see whether I was early, late, right or wrong.

The Call Watch flags races where the remaining vote can no longer realistically close the gap, adjusted for which way each state's late count usually breaks. It also flags races headed for an automatic recount or runoff.

Why count order matters

I got my start running absentee and early-vote programs in Michigan and Wisconsin, where elections are run at the municipal level and every clerk counts on their own schedule. The lesson stuck: the margin on the screen matters less than which votes are still out. Mail ballots, Election Day votes and big-city counts arrive at different times and lean different ways, and that's why an early lead can disappear by morning. The Race Caller tracks that for every state, so you know whether a lead is real.

TimesShown in Arizona time (MT), with Eastern time where it matters. On Election Day, Eastern is two hours ahead.

After the count

Once races are called, I'll publish a full post-game: how my picks held up against the polls, the markets and the forecasters, where the polls missed and why, and what actually moved the vote.
